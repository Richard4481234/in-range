InRange

Where your scores actually sit at every four-year college in the country.

Official figures from the U.S. Department of Education's College Scorecard — admission rates and the test scores of admitted students, reported by the colleges themselves. Not a survey, not scraped from anywhere, and not anyone's opinion. Enter a score and see where you fall.

What these numbers mean — and what they can't tell you

Where the data comes from

The College Scorecard publishes data that institutions report through IPEDS, the federal reporting system every accredited college is required to use. It covers essentially all of them, so there's no sampling and no volunteer bias. Run get_college_data.py to download it.

Admissions figures typically describe a cycle one to two years old — reporting takes time. Check the Dataset panel for the vintage of the file you loaded.

What "middle 50%" means

The range shown is the 25th to 75th percentile of admitted and enrolled students' scores. A quarter of admitted students scored below the bottom number. A quarter scored above the top one.

So it is not a cutoff. Being under the 25th percentile does not disqualify you, and being over the 75th does not admit you. It tells you what kind of academic company you'd be in.

Why there is no "chance me" percentage here

A tool that multiplies your score by an admit rate and hands back "you have a 23% chance" is making that number up. Admissions offices read essays, recommendations, course rigor, context, and their own institutional needs — none of which appear in this data or any public dataset.

So this shows you two separate facts and refuses to blend them: how selective the school is overall, and where your score sits among admitted students. You draw the conclusion.

How the Likely / Target / Reach label works

  • The starting point is the admit rate alone: 75%+ is Likely, 50–75% Target, 20–50% Reach.
  • Scoring above the 75th percentile shifts it one step easier. Below the 25th shifts it one step harder.
  • Any school admitting under 20% stays a reach no matter what you score. At those schools most rejected applicants are academically qualified, so a strong score buys far less than it appears to. A tool that calls Stanford a "target" because your SAT is 1560 is lying to you.
  • Schools that report no admit rate get no label. Blank is more honest than a guess.

What's missing

  • GPA. The federal data doesn't collect it, so this tool doesn't ask for it. Anyone showing you a GPA cutoff got it from a survey or made it up.
  • Major-level admissions. Engineering and nursing are often far harder than the university number suggests. These are institution-wide figures.
  • In-state versus out-of-state admit rates. At public universities the gap is often enormous. Only the combined rate is published here.
  • Early versus regular. Not reported at this level.
  • Test-optional schools' ranges now describe only those who submitted scores, which skews them upward.
